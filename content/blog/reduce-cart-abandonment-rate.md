To reduce cart abandonment rate on a lean team, skip the long tactic list. Start by finding the checkout step where your own shoppers leave. Next, shortlist the causes that fit that step, ship one fix, and measure it before changing anything else.

Aim at the share of abandonment you can fix, not at zero. [Baymard Institute](https://baymard.com/blog/ecommerce-checkout-usability-report-and-benchmark) puts the average cart abandonment rate at 70.19%, and in its survey 42% of US online shoppers said they had abandoned a cart within the last 3 months because they were just browsing or not ready to buy. Baymard calls that segment "almost impossible" to reduce through better checkout design, and says most of those shoppers leave before they even start checkout. The rest of this guide covers how to find your leak, weigh candidate fixes, and decide what goes first.

## What cart abandonment rate is normal, and how much of it can you fix?

Baymard has tracked the global average for 13 years, and says little has improved in those years. What it also says is that a lot of the remaining causes can be resolved, many of them through design changes alone. That second half is the part worth acting on.

Start with your own number rather than the average. Take completed purchases, divide by the number of carts, take that result away from one, and multiply by 100.

**Abandonment rate = (1 - completed purchases / carts) x 100**

The more useful split is not against a benchmark at all. It is between the abandonment you can address and the abandonment you cannot. Set the just-browsing share aside as a cost of doing business. What remains includes extra costs, trust, checkout length, site errors and payment options, and all of those are things you can change.

## Where in your checkout are shoppers actually dropping off?

Before choosing a fix, find the single step that loses the most shoppers. The output of this section is one named step, such as the shipping page or the payment page.

A [guide from the fulfilment firm Kirkwood](https://kirkwoodus.com/what-are-the-top-reasons-for-shopping-cart-abandonment/) names three things worth analysing:

- **The point in the process** where users abandon their carts
- **The frequency** of abandonment
- **The types of products** left behind

Lay out the checkout as a sequence of steps and note how many sessions reach each one. The step with the steepest fall is the one to work on first.

There is a low-cost check to run alongside the data, from the same guide: have friends and family shop on your site and work through checkout, then note what trips them up. It costs an afternoon and it catches the kind of problem analytics only shows you as a number.

## Which abandonment reasons match the step where you leak?

Once you have the leaking step, compare it with the reasons shoppers give. Baymard's 2025 survey of 1,026 US adults, published on [Statista](https://www.statista.com/statistics/1228452/reasons-for-abandonments-during-checkout-united-states/) with the just-browsing answer removed, gives these shares:

| Reason for abandoning | Share |
| --- | --- |
| High extra costs such as shipping, tax or fees | 39% |
| Delivery too slow | 21% |
| Site required an account | 19% |
| Didn't trust the site with card details | 19% |
| Checkout too long or complicated | 18% |
| Returns policy not satisfactory | 15% |
| Website errors or crashes | 15% |
| Couldn't see total cost up front | 14% |
| Too few payment methods | 10% |
| Card declined | 8% |

Pick the two or three reasons that could plausibly happen at your leaking step and set the others aside for now.

Note what sits at the top. Extra costs beat every other answer by a wide margin, and for most small stores that means shipping. If your leak is at the step where postage first appears, the fix may be a threshold rather than a redesign, and our [free shipping threshold calculator](/tools/free-shipping-threshold-calculator) will tell you what that threshold can afford to be.

One caution from the Statista notes: according to further Baymard research, the too-long-checkout answer should not be taken to mean that a single-page or single-step checkout fixes problems with checkout length.

## How do you estimate the revenue a checkout fix could recover?

Use your own traffic rather than an industry uplift figure. Gather three inputs from your analytics for each candidate fix:

1. Monthly sessions that reach the leaking step
2. The share of those sessions that leave at that step
3. Your average order value

Multiply sessions by the drop-off share to see how many shoppers leave, then apply a cautious and a hopeful recovery assumption and multiply by average order value. Our [conversion and revenue lift calculator](/tools/ecommerce-conversion-rate-calculator) does that arithmetic if you would rather not do it by hand. Treat the result as a range for comparing candidates, not as a forecast.

Why not use Baymard's headline number? Baymard's estimate is that an average large ecommerce site could lift its conversion rate by 35.26% through checkout design changes alone, a figure based on the checkout flows of leading sites such as Walmart and Amazon. Your store is not one of those sites. Baymard also says it finds small online retailers just as capable of crafting great checkout experiences if they stay nimble and focused, but that does not make the percentage your forecast.

## How much effort does each fix take without a developer?

This is the encouraging part. Baymard reports that the vast majority of the checkout changes in its study are related to page layout, the addition of simple form features, and improving microcopy, and so do not require advanced technical implementation or deep pockets.

Candidate fixes named in the sources:

| Fix | Source |
| --- | --- |
| Make the returns policy clear, easy to find and prominent in checkout | [Clarity Ventures](https://www.clarity-ventures.com/ecommerce/shopping-cart-abandonment-reasons-and-tips) |
| Make sure customers know what they will pay before entering payment information | Clarity Ventures |
| Offer a guest checkout option | Clarity Ventures |
| Make questions like "How did you hear about us?" optional | Kirkwood |
| Add PayPal, Apple Pay and buy-now-pay-later options | Kirkwood |

Form length is another target. Baymard's research indicates an ideal checkout can come down to 12 form elements, whereas the average US checkout displays 23.48 form elements by default, or 14.88 if only the form fields are counted. Most stores are carrying roughly twice the checkout they need.

How much work each fix takes depends on your platform and theme, so check that before ranking candidates.

## What order should a lean team fix things in?

Our decision rule: at the leaking step, ship the fix with the best estimated impact for the least effort, measure it against your baseline, then pick the next candidate.

Changing one thing at a time keeps results readable. If several changes go live together, it becomes hard to tell which one moved the number.

1. Name the leaking step
2. Shortlist two or three causes that fit it
3. Estimate recoverable orders for each
4. Take the low-effort, high-impact one first
5. Ship, measure that step, then repeat

Anything that does not address your leaking step waits.

![Five-step order for reducing cart abandonment: name the leaking checkout step, shortlist the causes that fit it, estimate recoverable orders from your own traffic, ship the lowest-effort fix, then measure that step before changing anything else.](/images/blog/cart-abandonment-fix-order.svg)

If the measurement comes back flat, the loop goes back to step two rather than forward to a second change. A fix that did not move the step is evidence about the cause, not a reason to pile on more fixes.

## Why might your abandonment rate not drop after a fix?

A sound fix can still show little movement. Some reasons to consider:

**Some abandoners were never going to buy.** Baymard calls the just-browsing segment "almost impossible" to reduce through better checkout design, and says most of these shoppers leave before checkout starts. Clarity Ventures adds that price researchers usually go through most of the process to see the total price, including shipping and taxes.

**The fix targeted the wrong cause.** If the step did not improve, go back to the reasons that fit that step rather than piling on changes.

**Discounts cut both ways.** Clarity Ventures notes that many shoppers look for coupon codes, and questions offering a code to customers who were going to purchase immediately anyway. Kirkwood suggests a 10 to 20% discount for comparison shoppers who abandon, so test offers carefully before making them permanent.

**Recovery emails are a backstop, not a fix.** Clarity Ventures advises that the first recovery email go out within an hour of abandonment, with follow-up emails over the next few days. That recovers some lost sales, but it does not repair the step that lost them.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a good cart abandonment rate?

There is no threshold that counts as good, and chasing the global average is the wrong target anyway. Baymard's tracked average sits at 70.19%, but a large share of that is people browsing rather than buying, which no checkout change will recover. The number worth improving is the drop-off at your own worst checkout step, measured against its own baseline.

### How do you calculate cart abandonment rate?

Divide completed purchases by the number of carts created in the same period, subtract that from one, and multiply by 100. The period matters more than the formula: use the same window each time, and avoid one that straddles a promotion or a seasonal peak, because a change in traffic mix will move the number on its own.

### Does a one-page checkout reduce abandonment?

Not on its own, and Baymard flags this directly. Its research warns that when shoppers say checkout was too long or complicated, that should not be read as evidence that a single-page or single-step checkout is the solution. The number of form elements shown is the more useful thing to reduce, and that is independent of how many pages they are spread across.

### Do abandoned cart emails actually work?

They recover some orders, and the common advice is to send the first within an hour and follow up over the next few days. Treat them as a backstop rather than a fix. An email that wins back a shopper who left because postage appeared late is cheaper to send than a checkout change, but the checkout is still losing everyone who does not open the email.

### How long should you wait before judging a checkout fix?

Long enough that the step has enough sessions to show a real difference, which for a small store usually means weeks rather than days. Use equal-length windows before and after, and write down anything running in that window that could move traffic mix. If you cannot say what the step's number was before the change, you cannot judge the change.
