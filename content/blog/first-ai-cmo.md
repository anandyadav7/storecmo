Nobody is the first AI CMO, in the sense the phrase implies. No public company has appointed an artificial intelligence as its chief marketing officer, given it budget authority, or made it accountable to a board. What exists instead are software products that use "first AI CMO" as a marketing line, and one genuinely interesting precedent from an adjacent role that tells you almost everything about how these announcements work.

That precedent is worth walking through, because it is the closest the business world has come, it was covered everywhere, and the detail that matters was buried in most of the coverage.

## What happened when a company did appoint an AI chief executive?

In August 2022, the Polish drinks company Dictador appointed an AI-powered humanoid robot named Mika as its **experimental** chief executive. Mika was built by Hanson Robotics, the firm behind the Sophia robot, and the appointment was reported worldwide. Mika gave interviews. It told Reuters it was "always on 24/7" and worked seven days a week, adding that its decision-making "relies on extensive data analysis and aligning with the company's strategic objectives" and is "devoid of personal bias".

Then there is the sentence that most coverage skipped past. Reporting the story, [Business Insider](https://www.businessinsider.com/humanoid-ai-robot-ceo-says-she-doesnt-have-weekends-2023-9) noted that Mika would not be firing any employees, because "the major significant decisions at Dictador will be made by human executives", according to Marek Szoldrowski, the company's European president, speaking to Reuters.

Read those two things together. The world's most famous AI executive appointment came with an explicit carve-out that the AI does not make the significant decisions. Its actual remit was helping spot potential clients, selecting artists to design bottles, and fronting a project involving a decentralised autonomous organisation and a collection of NFTs.

It was not even first. Business Insider notes that a Chinese gaming firm had already appointed an AI-powered virtual humanoid named Tang Yu as chief executive of its subsidiary Fujian NetDragon Websoft the year before.

So the pattern for AI C-suite appointments, established before anyone said "AI CMO" out loud, is this: the title is announced, the press covers it, and the authority stays where it was.

## Who is claiming the AI CMO title right now?

The claim has moved from robots to software, and at least two products currently market themselves this way.

IcyPluto brands itself "Cosmos' First AI CMO", though what it sells is a suite of separate marketing tools covering generative engine optimisation, translation, blog topics, video, ads and influencer outreach. Okara markets itself as "the AI CMO" and has been described in coverage as the world's first, and its published workflow is genuinely closer to the shape of the job: it researches a business first, produces strategy documents, and only then lets its agents execute against them.

We are naming these two because an article about a claim should say who is making it. We have not linked to either, and neither is a competitor we are trying to characterise unfairly. Okara in particular is doing something structurally sensible, and "strategy before execution" is the right instinct.

But look at what the title is being attached to. In both cases "AI CMO" describes **software you buy**, not an officer somebody appointed. That is a category difference, not a quibble, and it is why the question "who was first" cannot be answered: there is no register, no appointment, and no agreed definition of what would qualify. As we covered in [our field guide to the term](/blog/what-is-an-ai-cmo), the industry's own glossaries describe an AI CMO as a capability tier rather than a product category, which means "first" is a claim about branding rather than about achievement.

## Why does being first not settle anything?

Because "first" is a claim about chronology, and the thing you actually need to know is about authority.

A chief marketing officer is not a bundle of skills. It is a position with four things attached: a budget it can commit, decisions it can make without asking, accountability when those decisions are wrong, and a person who can overrule it. Software can have the first attribute in a limited way if you connect a payment method. It cannot have the third at all, because you cannot hold a subscription accountable. When something goes wrong, the accountable party is whoever approved it, which is you.

This is why the title claim is close to meaningless as a buying signal. It tells you a vendor chose an ambitious name. It tells you nothing about which decisions the product makes, on what evidence, or what happens when it is wrong.

## What would actually count as a first?

If you want a test that separates a real milestone from a press release, here is the one we would use. A genuine first AI CMO would need all four.

1. **Named delegated decisions.** Not "sets strategy" but a written list: this system decides the weekly promotion, up to this discount depth, within these categories. If nobody can produce the list, nothing has been delegated.
2. **Budget authority with a ceiling.** The ability to commit real money without a human approving each transaction, bounded by a limit somebody set deliberately.
3. **A named accountable human.** Someone whose job is affected when the system's decisions are bad. This is the one no vendor announcement has ever included, and its absence is the tell.
4. **Reversal rights that get used.** A documented case of a human overriding the system, and evidence the override was not treated as a malfunction. A system nobody has ever overruled is a system nobody is really supervising.

By that standard, the Dictador appointment scores one out of four at best, and every software product claiming the title scores zero, because none of them is a position in an organisation. That is not a criticism of the software. It is a reason to ignore the title and read the specification.

## What does this mean if you run a store?

Practically, three things.

**Ignore the title entirely as a buying signal.** Ask instead which decisions the product makes without you, and what its reasoning looks like when it does. A vendor who can answer that specifically is telling you something. A vendor leaning on "first" or "only" is telling you about their positioning.

**Expect the carve-out.** Every honest system in this category keeps significant decisions human, exactly as Dictador did. If a vendor does not volunteer where that line sits, ask, and be wary of an answer that cannot name a single decision the system will not make.

**Judge it on your own decisions.** Write down the three marketing calls you keep postponing, then ask whether the system can show its reasoning on those, using your numbers. That test is far more informative than any claim about being first, and we go through it in more depth in [whether you should let an AI CMO decide](/blog/should-you-let-an-ai-cmo-decide).

## Frequently asked questions

### Who was the first AI CMO?

There is no documented first, because no company has appointed an AI to the position of chief marketing officer. Several software products market themselves as the first or only AI CMO, which is a branding claim about a tool rather than a record of an appointment. The nearest real precedent is in the chief executive role, not marketing.

### Has an AI ever actually been made a company officer?

In name, twice that are well documented. NetDragon Websoft appointed a virtual humanoid called Tang Yu as chief executive of a subsidiary, and in August 2022 Dictador appointed the robot Mika as its experimental chief executive. In the Dictador case the company stated that significant decisions would still be made by human executives, so the appointment transferred the title rather than the authority.

### Is "AI CMO" a real job title?

Not in any regulated or standardised sense. There is no register of AI officers, no professional body that recognises the role, and no agreed definition of what a system must do to qualify. Anyone can use the phrase, which is precisely why "first" carries so little information.

### Should I take a "first AI CMO" claim seriously when buying?

Treat it as neutral. It is neither a red flag nor a credential. The questions that matter are which decisions the system makes unsupervised, whether it shows the reasoning behind them, what its budget authority is, and who is accountable when it is wrong. A vendor comfortable answering those does not need the superlative.

### Will an AI ever hold a real CMO role?

Parts of it, plausibly, and sooner in the analysis and prioritisation layers than in brand judgment. The obstacle is not capability, it is accountability: legal and governance structures assume a person is answerable for executive decisions. Until that changes, an AI can do a great deal of a CMO's work without holding the office.

## Next step

The next time you see the claim, do one thing: ask the vendor to name a single decision their system makes that you would not see before it happens. Then ask what happens when it is wrong.

Most of the time there is no such decision, which means you are being offered analysis and production rather than leadership, and that is genuinely useful as long as you are paying for what it is.
