Judge a lead magnet by the revenue of the list it builds, measured per email address, not by how many signups the popup collects. Run that test and the blanket "10% off your first order" popup can lose to something narrower: an offer that only a buyer of a specific product would want.

The reason is arithmetic, not taste. A percentage-off code costs you margin on every order that uses it, including the orders you were going to get anyway. Made With Intent, a vendor selling real-time intent software, reports in its [Intent Gap report](https://www.madewithintent.ai/articles/how-to-get-started-with-intent-based-discounting) that 83% of online shoppers say they have used a discount code when they would have paid full price.

## What counts as a lead magnet for an ecommerce store?

A lead magnet in ecommerce is the perk a store offers a visitor in exchange for permission to market to them: a discount code, early access, a back-in-stock alert, a quiz result.

Two things are being bought at once, and it is easy to price only one of them. The first is the email address or phone number. The second is consent, which is what makes the address worth anything later.

The container matters less than most lists of ideas suggest. Whether the offer sits in a popup, an inline form or a footer embed does not change whether it attracts someone who wanted your product or someone who wanted a coupon.

## Why can a 10%-off welcome popup lose money?

Because the discount is paid on every redemption, while the incremental orders are only some of them. [Nebulab](https://nebulab.com/blog/reduce-discounts-without-losing-revenue), a vendor that consults for DTC brands on pricing and discounting, works the margin at list prices: take a product priced at $100 with $55 of cost of goods, a 45% gross margin. Cut the price 30% to $70, and cost stays at $55, so gross profit per unit falls from $45 to $15. To keep the same gross profit, a store must now sell three units for every one it used to sell at full price.

A 10% welcome code is gentler, but the structure is identical, and it is handed to the one group most likely to buy anyway: people already on your product pages. Set that beside shoppers' own admission that they would often have paid full price (the 83% figure above), and a share of your welcome-flow "revenue" is margin you refunded to buyers.

There is a second cost no dashboard shows. Nebulab argues that a discounted price becomes the customer's new anchor, so the next full price reads as a markup, and that customers acquired on deep discounts buy less often, churn faster, and generate lower lifetime value than full-price cohorts. Before changing anything, Nebulab recommends pulling 12 months of orders and calculating three numbers: discount penetration rate (share of orders using a code), revenue at risk (share of revenue from discounted orders), and average discount depth.

## How do you measure a lead magnet by revenue per subscriber?

Divide the revenue the list produces by the number of addresses it contains, over a fixed window, and subtract the discount you gave away to get them. Signup rate goes in a second column, not the first.

A worked example, with every number an assumption. Say an exit-intent popup collects 500 new subscribers in a month, and those subscribers produce $1,100 of attributed revenue. Revenue per subscriber is $1,100 / 500 = $2.20. Now the offer cost: say 60 of the 500 redeem the 10% code on a $100 order, giving away $10 each, $600 in total. After the discount, the list is worth $1,100 - $600 = $500, or $1.00 per address. The signup rate did not move, but the number worth reporting fell by more than half. If you are not sure what order value to assume, this site's [average order value calculator](/tools/average-order-value-calculator) works it out from your own revenue and order count.

If you use Klaviyo, most of this is already in the tool. Its [form revenue metrics](https://help.klaviyo.com/hc/en-us/articles/27695838219675) show total revenue, orders and average order value for each website sign-up form.

On signup rate itself, [Klaviyo's sign-up form analytics guide](https://help.klaviyo.com/hc/en-us/articles/360015960712) recommends aiming for about 3% or higher on popups and flyouts, and says in the same breath to look past that at subscriber quality: welcome-series engagement and the conversion rates of those messages. That is the right order. A 6% submit rate on a coupon popup and a 1.5% rate on a back-in-stock alert can easily rank the other way on revenue per address.

## Which lead magnets self-select buyers instead of freebie hunters?

The ones tied to a specific product or a specific moment.

Our view: for a small catalog, the strongest lead magnet is usually a notification rather than an offer, because it costs no margin and it arrives with the reason to buy already attached.

When an incentive is still needed, Nebulab's alternatives cost less than a straight percentage off. Store credit or loyalty points keep the reference price intact: a $100 product stays $100 and the customer holds $30 to spend next time, and because not everyone redeems, the real cost is lower. Value-adds carry high perceived value at low actual cost: free shipping, an extended return window, a gift using slow-moving stock, or early access to drops. And if you issue cash discounts, Nebulab suggests retiring site-wide codes and homepage sale banners and sending unique, single-use codes to narrow audiences instead: lapsed customers, cart abandoners, first-time visitors who did not convert. The same costing applies to word of mouth: see [how to rank referral incentive ideas by margin](/blog/referral-incentive-ideas).

## Which form settings and targeting rules catch teams off guard?

Two mechanics in Klaviyo are easy to plan around and then discover you cannot use. According to [Klaviyo's getting started guide for sign-up forms](https://help.klaviyo.com/hc/en-us/articles/360026474752), a banner form can be configured so that clicking it opens another of your live forms, but that option is not available for embedded forms. Teasers, the small handle that lets someone reopen a form they closed, are available for popup, flyout and full page forms, not for banner forms.

Targeting is also narrower than it looks in the EU, EEA, UK and Switzerland. The same guide says that, depending on a store's Shopify customer privacy settings, Klaviyo may not track onsite events for visitors there who have not consented, and so cannot apply certain types of form targeting to them. Build offer timing and audience rules around what the platform can actually see, not around an assumed default.

## What does the capture layer cost on tools a lean team already owns?

Close to nothing. Shopify Forms is free in the [Shopify App Store](https://apps.shopify.com/shopify-forms), where it holds a 4.5 rating across 776 reviews. Shopify's [Forms page](https://www.shopify.com/forms) lists popup and inline forms, discount codes that apply automatically at checkout for Shop Pay users, and a forms dashboard showing how visitors engage with each form, so you can use the metrics to improve future campaigns. Check that dashboard after the first week live. A form that needs logic beyond its editor is a job for a developer, not a setting.

Maintenance is the hidden cost, not licensing. Every lead magnet added is another form to target, another segment to watch and another welcome message to keep accurate. Three capture points reviewed regularly beat eight forgotten ones, the same logic this site applies to channel choice in [the complete ecommerce marketing strategy for lean teams](/blog/ecommerce-marketing-strategy-for-lean-teams).

## How do you keep consent clean so the list stays worth sending to?

Collect consent explicitly, per channel, and let the email platform handle confirmation itself. Klaviyo's getting started guide calls collecting email and SMS consent on separate steps of a multi-step form best practice. Its [developer guide to integrating forms platforms](https://developers.klaviyo.com/en/docs/guide_to_integrating_a_forms_and_popups_platform) adds that SMS consent should be kept separate from email consent rather than bundled under one pre-checked box, and that consent should only be recorded when the visitor took an explicit action.

The list's own opt-in setting decides whether a signup is subscribed immediately or enters a pending state until the person confirms. The same developer guide tells integrators not to build a parallel confirmation that duplicates Klaviyo's own opt-in message. If a developer is building you a custom signup form, send them that guide first.

## Where does this revenue-first approach fail?

Mostly in measurement, and in moving too fast. Klaviyo notes that its form revenue data only exists from June 5, 2024 onward, and is not shown for in-app forms, back-in-stock forms or landing pages, or supported for tap-to-text forms. That rules out attribution for one of the higher-intent magnets above. Cookie consent tools and some ad blockers also limit the onsite events behind form analytics. And since April 1, 2025, Klaviyo has filtered bot and crawler activity out of form analytics, which lowered viewed-form counts and raised submit rates, so a rate compared across that date is not like for like.

The bigger failure is strategic. A store that already runs on codes cannot simply pull the welcome discount and keep the margin. Nebulab's estimate is that brands with 70% or more of orders using a code and deep average discounts typically need 12 to 18 months to fully recover pricing power, while brands under 40% penetration can sometimes move within a single quarter. Nebulab also says to expect revenue to stay flat or dip slightly in the short term, as the mix shifts toward full-price buyers.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a lead magnet in ecommerce?

A lead magnet in ecommerce is the perk a store offers a visitor in exchange for consent to market to them, such as a discount code, early access, a back-in-stock alert or a quiz result. The form itself is just the container that collects the address and the consent; the magnet is what earns them.

### Is a welcome discount bad for an ecommerce store?

Not inherently, but it is expensive and often paid to people who would have bought anyway. Nebulab's margin example shows a 30% discount on a $100 product with $55 of cost of goods dropping per-unit profit from $45 to $15, and Made With Intent reports that 83% of shoppers say they have used a discount code when they would have paid full price. Store credit, free shipping or early access deliver perceived value without resetting your reference price.

### What is a good signup rate for an ecommerce popup?

Klaviyo recommends aiming for a form submit rate of about 3% or higher on popups and flyouts, then judging subscribers on welcome-series engagement and the conversion rate of those messages rather than the submit rate alone. Rank forms on revenue per subscriber first, and use submit rate only to explain why the ranking came out the way it did.

### Do you need a paid email tool to run a lead magnet?

No. Shopify Forms is free, and Shopify's Forms page says it works with Shopify's own free automation and messaging tools, including welcome emails. A paid email platform buys deeper segmentation and per-form revenue reporting, not the ability to collect a signup and follow up on it.

## Next step

Pick the single form that collects the most signups today and calculate its revenue per subscriber for last month: attributed revenue divided by addresses collected, minus the discount given away on redeemed codes. Then build one competing magnet with no discount in it, for example a back-in-stock alert on the product that goes out of stock most often, and compare the two on that same number over the next 30 days.

If you use Klaviyo, set the revenue lookback window before you start. Klaviyo's default is 2 hours, and the setting applies to every website form in the account, including reports on past activity, so changing it later changes historical revenue totals.
