Decide with three of your own numbers, not a pros-and-cons list: margin left after each channel's fees, how often customers buy a second time, and how many marketing hours you actually have each week. If the category referral fee on Amazon costs less than what you currently pay to acquire an order on your own site, and buyers rarely reorder, the marketplace deserves the next hour. If people reorder and someone can run email and product pages, your own store does.

The fee math is public on both sides: Amazon charges a referral fee plus a selling-plan fee, while Shopify charges a flat plan fee plus card processing. The full numbers for both are in the comparison below, so you can run your own subtraction rather than guess.

## Which channel should get your next hour and dollar?

Run two contribution-per-order calculations and compare them against one number: what an order costs you to acquire on your own site.

**On Amazon**, start with the selling price, subtract product cost, then subtract the category referral fee and fulfillment. [Amazon's standard selling fees](https://sell.amazon.com/pricing) are a referral fee charged as a percentage of the total price or a minimum amount, whichever is greater, with a $0.30 minimum in most categories. The percentage is not uniform: Home and Kitchen, Pet Supplies, Toys and Games and Sports and Outdoors are 15%, while other categories are tiered by price, so look up your product's own fee category. Then add the plan: $0.99 per item on the Individual plan, or $39.99 a month on Professional.

**On your own store**, the platform takes less and you pay for demand instead. On [Shopify's Basic plan](https://www.shopify.com/pricing), online standard card rates are 2.9% + 30¢, in-person card rates 2.6% + 10¢, and third-party transaction fees are 2% if you process with another provider rather than Shopify Payments. [Shopify's own documentation](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/intro-to-shopify/pricing-plans/pricing-overview) is explicit that orders processed through Shopify Payments, Shop Pay and Shop Pay Installments are not charged those third-party transaction fees, so for most small stores the realistic platform cost is the card rate plus the Basic plan fee: $29 USD/mo paid yearly, or $39 USD/mo paid monthly.

Now the comparison that decides it. If a 15% referral fee on a $40 order is $6.00, and it costs you $14 in ads to sell that same item on your own site, Amazon is buying demand more cheaply than you are, and your next dollar belongs there. Reverse the two numbers and your store wins. Our view: most lean teams never write this subtraction down, so the channel that feels busy wins instead of the channel that pays.

Before you plug prices into that subtraction, check that the price itself survives it; our guide to [ecommerce pricing that protects profit per order](/blog/ecommerce-pricing-strategy) works through the payback a price has to clear.

## How do Amazon and your own website compare side by side?

The same five criteria, scored for each channel.

| | Amazon marketplace | Your own website (Shopify) |
|---|---|---|
| Fees that hit margin | Referral fee by category, plus a selling-plan fee, plus fulfillment | A flat monthly plan fee, plus card processing rates |
| Customer relationship | Brand tools via Brand Registry (A+ Content, Stores, Brand Analytics, Vine) | Customer accounts with persistent sign-in, plus email and abandoned-checkout tools on every plan |
| Where demand comes from | Amazon's existing shoppers | Traffic you build or buy |
| Weekly hours to run it well | Listings, category and title compliance, pricing, ads | Site, email, content, ads, merchandising |
| Setup and compliance overhead | Category listing rules, and Brand Registry needs a trademark | Store, payments, policies |

Read the table as cost of entry against cost of demand. Amazon charges a share of every sale and hands you shoppers. Your own site charges less per sale and hands you the bill for attention.

Google Shopping and TikTok Shop are not neutral alternatives to either of these; both still run on top of a store or a feed. A separate comparison already works through how [TikTok Shop](/blog/is-tiktok-shop-worth-it), Google Shopping and Amazon compare on cost and effort for teams weighing those two specifically.

## Who owns the customer after the sale?

Your own site owns the repeat-purchase mechanics outright, and that is the strongest argument for it when customers reorder.

On your own store, [a customer profile is created automatically](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/customers/customer-accounts) the first time someone signs in with an email address that is not already tied to one. That is the whole mechanism: the address arrives as a side effect of the sale, and it is yours to use next month.

On Amazon, the brand-side equivalents arrive through [Brand Registry](https://sell.amazon.com/blog/what-is-amazon-brand-registry): A+ Content on listings, a Brand Store, Brand Analytics and Amazon Vine for reviews. Those are merchandising and measurement tools, not a list you can email.

Repeat purchase rate is the number that breaks the tie between the two. A product bought once a year rewards whoever wins the first sale cheapest; a consumable bought every six weeks rewards whoever can email the buyer [a review request](/blog/review-request-email-template) and a reorder prompt, which is only you.

## What does Amazon require before your first sale?

One piece of setup carries a hard deadline before you can trade on a brand name: Brand Registry.

[Amazon's brand enrollment guide](https://sell.amazon.com/blog/brand-registry-requirements) says enrollment requires an active registered trademark or a pending trademark registration, as a text-based word mark or an image-based mark with words, letters or numbers, from approved government intellectual property offices. The brand name has to be permanently affixed to the product or packaging in your photos: printing, sewing, laser etching or engraving count, while stickers, labels, stamps, tags, stock images and computer-generated mockups do not. After you submit, Amazon emails the trademark correspondent to confirm the brand association, and that person has 7 days to respond or the application is declined and must be resubmitted.

That deadline is the part worth planning around. It depends on someone outside your team, often a lawyer who filed the mark months ago, noticing an email within a week.

## What does your own website require before the traffic arrives?

A store is only half of it. The other half is the product data that search and shopping channels read, and that is where lean stores stall. Neither half generates visits on its own: a working checkout and a clean feed make you eligible for demand, they do not create it.

For Google Shopping and free listings, a title is required for every product. Google's [title attribute documentation](https://support.google.com/merchants/answer/6324415?hl=en) sets the limit at 1 to 150 characters, recommends using all 150, and says users will usually notice only the first 70 or fewer characters of your title, depending on screen size, so put the most important details first. One rule in that documentation catches small catalogs most often: add the distinguishing details of each variant to the title, and submit those details through the matching attributes as well, such as color, size or age group. The title says "Small, Black"; the feed still has to say it in the fields, too.

## When does running both channels split a small team too thin?

When neither channel gets a weekly owner. A one- or two-person team can genuinely run one channel well, maintain a second, and will quietly neglect a third.

The operational cost of running both is not only attention. The expensive part is that Amazon listings and your own product pages drift apart: different titles, different prices, different photos, two sets of compliance rules, and two review inboxes.

A third channel makes that split obvious, and adds an extra vendor relationship for a team that may not have a spare afternoon.

Our view: add a second channel only when the first one runs on a routine you could hand to someone else in writing.

## Where could this comparison lead you wrong?

Four ways, worth checking before you act on it.

The fee math goes stale. According to Amazon's seller pricing page, referral percentages are set by category and by price tier, and new-seller incentives ([Amazon advertises over $50,000](https://sell.amazon.com/pricing) in credits, bonuses and exclusive benefits for a new-to-Amazon brand, including referral-fee waivers in the first year) can make that first year look structurally cheaper than it will be once the waivers end. Model the un-incentivized number before you commit inventory.

Acquisition cost is harder to pin down than referral fees. A referral fee is a published percentage; your cost per order is an attribution question. If you are reading one platform's number as the whole truth, our piece on first click vs [last click attribution for small stores](/blog/first-click-vs-last-click-attribution) explains why the same order can be credited twice.

The comparison also treats one seller's result as a benchmark too easily. Amazon's pricing page quotes Dewar Gaines, founder of Gaines Family Farmstead, saying it is "more than 50% cheaper for us to acquire a new customer through Amazon than it is elsewhere." That is one seller's figure, in one category, on Amazon's own marketing page. Knowing the brand is the point: ask yourself how much your catalogue resembles theirs before borrowing the number.

And capacity beats arithmetic. If the subtraction says your own store wins but nobody has two hours a week for email and product pages, the store does not actually win. It just stops being measured.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does the Amazon vs own website decision actually come down to?

Amazon versus your own website is a choice about who pays for demand: the marketplace charges a referral fee on every sale and supplies the shoppers, while your own store charges a flat platform fee and leaves acquisition entirely to you. Compare the category referral fee plus fulfillment cost against what an order currently costs you to acquire on your own site, then weigh in your repeat purchase rate and the weekly hours you actually have before moving budget.

### Is Amazon cheaper than running your own store?

Per order it can be, and the answer depends on your category's referral fee against what an order costs you to acquire on your own site. Amazon's referral fee runs 15% in categories like Home and Kitchen and is tiered by price in others, on top of a selling-plan fee of $0.99 per item or $39.99 a month. Shopify's entry plan costs $29/mo paid yearly with card rates from 2.9% + 30¢. The platform fee is lower on your own site, so the real comparison is Amazon's referral fee against your advertising cost per order.

### Can you keep the customer relationship when selling on Amazon?

Not directly: Amazon's Brand Registry gives sellers brand-side tools, including A+ Content, a Brand Store, Brand Analytics and Amazon Vine, but none of them hand you a customer's email address. Repeat-purchase marketing, built from email lists and persistent customer accounts, lives on your own site instead.

### Should a two-person team run Amazon and its own store at once?

Only when the first channel already runs on a routine that could be handed to someone else in writing. Running both means maintaining two sets of titles, prices and compliance rules at once, and our view is that the hours this drift consumes, not the platform fees, are what decide whether a two-person team can carry a second channel.

## Next step

Open a spreadsheet and work through one product, not the whole catalog. Column A: selling price. Column B: product cost. Column C: the referral fee for that product's real fee category, using the tier that matches its price, plus the plan fee divided by expected monthly units, plus fulfillment. Column D: the same price minus product cost, minus your card processing rate, minus shipping, minus your current cost per acquired order. Whichever column leaves more is where this month's hour and dollar go. Run it for your best seller and your slowest mover, because they often disagree.
